AI for public good

Where can AI make public services work better?

Four ways to test whether AI helps deliver a service people need.

By Grayson Thomas · November 22, 2026

Flood warnings Vaccination Language access Learning

AI earns its place when it helps a service reach someone who would otherwise miss it, gives people useful time to act, or improves a result they care about at a cost the operator can sustain. Four examples illustrate what to test. The evidence ranges from work with real households and classrooms to early usability studies and proposals that still need field evaluation.

A sharper flood forecast matters only if a warning gets through and people can act. A better map of missed vaccinations helps only if a team can deliver a reliable clinic. A translation earns trust when people can understand it and use it to complete a real task. A tutor is useful when students can show what they learned after the tool is gone.

A fair comparison

For each use, begin with a staffed service, a clear decision and a simpler method that can realistically do the same job. Check four things before investing.

  • Burden and reach. How many people face this specific barrier, in which places, and how many could actually use the proposed service?
  • Evidence. Has a study shown an improvement in the public outcome, a change in delivery, or just a better score on a model test? Each supports a different claim.
  • Costs and practical access. Count staff review, devices, connectivity, translation, training and maintenance. Ask what an equivalent investment in the current service could achieve.
  • Risk and accountability. Name who can approve outputs, fix a mistake or shut the tool down. Define what happens when the model is unavailable. Test results for groups the system might routinely miss. Use public, aggregate or simulated inputs for early tests.

The projects below are proposals unless an evaluation is cited. A credible test keeps time and resources comparable and measures both benefit and harm. Some public services will gain more from better staffing, standardized records or simpler delivery. Flood warnings people can act on

An earlier flood forecast can give people time to move medication, check on neighbors or get to shelter. That time matters only if the warning reaches them and they have a way to act. In November 2026, the UN Office for Disaster Risk Reduction and the World Meteorological Organization reported that only 64 of 136 countries reporting multi-hazard warning systems described capabilities in all four areas, from understanding risk to preparing a response. UNDRR and WMO, Global Status 2026

Where AI can help. A peer-reviewed 2024 study tested Google’s river-flow model at gauges withheld from training. At five days’ lead, it performed better or comparably on detection of 1-, 2- and 5-year river-flow events, compared with Copernicus GloFAS predictions for the day they were issued. The result is a historical river forecast comparison across basins. It leaves open how much usable warning time a particular community would gain. Nearing et al., Nature

Where a simpler method can match it. A November 2026 study at one gauge in Ghana’s White Volta Basin found that a machine-learning ensemble beat a global benchmark that omitted a major upstream reservoir. In the local comparison, carrying forward the latest observed river flow did just as well at one day, and a linear model with the same inputs slightly outperformed the ensemble on high-flow days at three and five days. The authors proposed a warning system based on their results. They did not evaluate a live rollout or effects on residents. Obeng et al., Natural Hazards and Earth System Sciences, table 2 and §5.6.3

What reached people. In a randomized study across 319 communities in Bihar, India, all communities had access to Google flood forecasts. Researchers added trained volunteers who relayed warnings by loudspeaker, flags and messaging apps in 160 communities. In 2023, 40% of households in communities with volunteer outreach reported receiving an alert, compared with 23% in the comparison group. The study also found fewer reported floods without an alert and more alerts without a reported flood. In the highest-alert group of communities, estimated household illness-treatment spending was about one-third lower. These preliminary working-paper results measure the added outreach, with the same forecast available on both sides. They leave the independent effect of AI on household outcomes open. Jagnani and Pande, figures 7 and 11 and table 4

A practical first test. Run one closed tabletop exercise with a willing Saline County partner. Use archived National Weather Service alerts and Saline River gauge data to replay how a flood notice reaches a pantry, school or community organization. The baseline is the official alert, a fixed local checklist and short messages translated and reviewed in advance. Test AI only against a specific gap, such as whether an additional forecast provides useful time or an AI-assisted translation is faster with equal comprehension. Keep the test offline and let the authorized alerting agencies control real warnings. NWS alerts documentation · USGS Saline River gauge · WMO Common Alerting Protocol

Measure how much earlier people get the notice, how many can explain and carry out the right action in the drill, and who misses the message without a smartphone. For a forecast test, count missed flood events and false alarms alongside minutes gained. For a translation test, check place names, timing and instructions against the official alert with local-language reviewers. Track operator time and cost. A model belongs in the workflow only when it improves one of those outcomes safely. UNDRR, WMO, ITU and IFRC guidance on AI in early warning

Restore missed childhood vaccination sessions

On July 15, 2026, WHO and UNICEF estimated that 13.5 million infants went without a first dose of a diphtheria, tetanus and pertussis-containing vaccine in 2025, compared with 12.8 million in 2019. Missing that first dose, called DTP1, is the operational measure of zero-dose status. It indicates a gap in routine immunization and cannot establish a child's complete vaccine history. National estimates help show the scale of the problem. District teams need more current information to decide which outreach sessions need attention. WHO/UNICEF coverage release, WHO indicator definition

The evidence favors reliable delivery

In a randomized trial across 134 villages in rural Rajasthan, 6% of children aged one to three were fully immunized under usual services, compared with 18% where reliable monthly clinics were added and 39% where the clinics also offered small in-kind incentives. A later randomized study in Haryana tested 75 outreach combinations. Its 2025 analysis estimated that a selected bundle of local information networks, reminders and incentives increased recorded measles vaccinations by 44% after adjusting for selection of the best-performing program. These trials measured the delivery packages and used outcomes other than first doses of DTP. The benefit of adding an AI tool to district planning remains unmeasured. Rajasthan trial, Haryana trial and analysis

Test one planning task

Work with a district immunization team and community representatives to verify a specific bottleneck. A useful candidate is an outreach session a facility cancels because of staff absence, stock shortages, or a transport problem. The team may need days to discover that the session has no replacement. Confirm that this delay happens and that the district has staff and supplies to respond before building anything.

  1. Start with the rule-based version. Use the existing district information system or a spreadsheet export with aggregate counts and session status. Flag planned sessions without a recorded completion or replacement, stock-out days, missing facility reports and denominator inconsistencies. For example, a canceled session with a verified reason and no make-up date is actionable. A missing report calls for a reporting check. Coverage above 100% calls for reconciliation. WHO notes that up to 26% of administrative entities in its 2025 collection reported DTP3 coverage above 100%, reflecting problems such as population estimates and people crossing district boundaries for care. DHIS2 already supports immunization planning and several of these input types. WHO district data limitations, DHIS2 Health Data Toolkit
  2. Test AI only where notes cause extra work. Give the model redacted, approved facility summaries. Ask it to extract the cancellation reason and show the exact supporting text, or record that the reason is unknown. The comparison is the same spreadsheet with and without this extraction. Test on held-out historical periods or in parallel with normal operations. An illustrative success threshold would be 20% less reviewer time at the same rate of identifying independently confirmed urgent issues. Count false flags, missed urgent cases, disagreement across languages, and cases from facilities that report infrequently. District staff verify every recommendation before acting.
  3. Measure children reached separately. If the tool saves time without degrading accuracy, compare AI-assisted planning with rules-only planning across enough clinic catchments, using a phased randomized rollout where feasible. Keep comparable outreach capacity and measure the verified number of resident infants receiving a documented first DTP-containing dose per 1,000 eligible infants. Audit a sample of records for duplicates, age attribution and late reporting. Report the share of canceled sessions restored, added costs, staff time and results for harder-to-reach catchments. Improvement in document processing alone is an operational result.

Keep child records and identifiable session notes out of the model. Retain approval and correction logs with the program owner, restrict access to internal site information, and publish only aggregates that cannot expose a small community or sensitive location. Caregivers and health workers should be able to report false flags and challenge changes. The public contribution can be an open data dictionary, a synthetic test set, the rules baseline and a preregistered comparison protocol. Release field results with the health authority and communities' approval, including null effects and reporting gaps. WHO 2026 AI-related health research oversight, UNICEF guidance on AI and children

Help people find public services in the language they use

A person who needs a public service may know what they are trying to do without knowing the program’s official name. They should be able to ask in a familiar language and get a correct next step. Voice can help when forms are hard to read, but it still needs a route that works on basic phones or in person. The International Telecommunication Union estimates that 2 billion people remained offline in 2026. Internet use reached 32% in low-income countries. ITU Facts and Figures 2026

In a 2025 usability study in India, 15 low-income workers tried both a voice-first AI prototype and the form-based version of the government’s myScheme app using the same scheme data. The researchers randomized app order. All 15 reached a list of schemes with the prototype, compared with nine using myScheme. Mean times among those who reached a list were 49 seconds (95% CI 26–72) and 118 seconds (81–155), respectively, excluding waits for data. The small convenience sample mostly spoke Telugu, which the comparison app lacked at the time. The study changed language support, voice, and navigation together and measured scheme discovery. It leaves open whether AI itself helped and whether anyone was more likely to finish an application or receive a benefit. The current myScheme FAQ reports web content in 15 languages and a chatbot in Hindi and English. Original usability study, sections 3 and 4.2 · Current myScheme FAQ

Translation quality still needs local checks. The AFRIDOC-MT team evaluated models against human translations in five African languages and found that models sometimes returned the wrong language or produced repeated or incomplete text. Performance varied by model and language. An average benchmark score cannot tell a service office whether a caller in a particular dialect understood where to go. AFRIDOC-MT, sections 5–6

How to test it

Start with one service office and one language that residents identify as underserved. Pay local speakers and translators to check a small set of public answers about office hours, documents and where to get help. Publish the approved text and recorded audio, with a staff phone or in-person route. In the AI version, use the model to match a spoken or typed question to the same approved answer. Let staff handle questions the system cannot match. The office keeps responsibility for updating facts and deciding eligibility.

Use fictional cases and volunteer participants. Randomly assign them to the translated menu or the same service with AI question matching. Keep the answer set, languages and human fallback the same. Have an evaluator who does not know which version participants used score whether each person can identify the correct next step within an agreed time. Include network delays and report results by language variety where sample sizes permit. Agree to a minimum improvement in correct task completion before testing and track harmful instructions, staff time and cost per correct answer. A material error involving a deadline, fee or required document pauses the test. If the translated menu performs as well, use it. A small, controlled test can justify a further trial. Real changes in application completion and access require follow-up with the service office and community.

Help students learn with supervised practice

UNESCO’s 2026 Global Education Monitoring Report estimates 273 million children and young people were out of school in 2024. This counts school access. It is distinct from learning outcomes among students who attend. Tutoring tools can help when students have access to lessons and support. Expanding school places, safety, staffing and transport addresses barriers that software cannot remove. UNESCO access and equity report

What has been measured. In Edo, Nigeria, first-year senior secondary students were randomized to an offer of six weeks of teacher-guided after-school sessions using an AI tutor or to usual schooling. The combined English, digital skills and AI skills test improved by 0.31 standard deviations (standard error 0.068). The English component improved by 0.238 standard deviations (standard error 0.068). The paper reports about $48 per participant in pilot costs, including development and delivery. The trial measures the whole package, including extra time and teacher guidance. It does not tell us how much the model added to an equally long, teacher-guided alternative. World Bank working paper, Tables 2, 3 and 13

A separate randomized high-school math study in Türkiye found that unrestricted use of a GPT-based assistant improved performance during supported practice but reduced scores on a subsequent unassisted exam by 17% relative to controls. A teacher-designed tutor that gave hints largely avoided the drop, with no detectable exam benefit. Those results show why success during use and later independent learning need separate measures. Bastani and colleagues, PNAS

An incremental test. A school and its teachers can use one curriculum unit and compare the same amount of supervised practice. One group gets teacher-written examples and graduated hints. The other gets the same materials with a constrained tutor that responds with approved hints and routes uncertain answers to the teacher. Include a usable offline option and log who misses sessions and why. Use an unassisted assessment several weeks later and record teacher load, cost per pupil, material errors, and gaps in participation. The school sets the minimum learning gain worth paying for and reviews the results before expanding. Any digital tool handling student data needs school approval and appropriate safeguards. UNESCO guidance for generative AI in education

What to publish after a test

Before starting, record who owns the workflow, who is eligible to participate, which baseline the new method must beat, the outcome and the conditions that would pause deployment. Report the cost and all intended outcomes, including false alerts, unresolved requests or people missed. Keep simple fallbacks working during the test.

Afterward, publish the protocol, aggregate results, limits and adjustments in a versioned public record. Include enough detail for another team to reproduce the comparison, using synthetic examples or approved aggregate data. Report how the result differs by language, location or other relevant group when sample sizes allow. Protect privacy when they do not. A null result can show a government or nonprofit where its next dollar would be better spent.
